Live Multiplayer

Electric Poker

Responsible for

  • Led UX for a multi-team live poker build, scoping from a 9-player concept down to a 3-player tournament format
  • Ran usability and perception testing with potential customers, then reworked pace of play and player seat layout from the results
  • Owned front-end design for the games team, partnering with art on layout and formatting for implementation
  • Contributed to a USPTO design patent covering aspects of the interface

Electric Poker was a long cross-functional effort across many teams at DraftKings and our first multiplayer online game. The games team owned all front-end design and art. We originally scoped an up-to-9-player game with a much larger feature set — a genuinely difficult test of maximizing screen space for a mobile-portrait-first experience with that many simultaneous seats.

Due to project timelines and scope, we reduced the experience to a “last man standing” tournament format: 3-player tournaments with ranging grand prizes. The “Electric” theming comes from the pace — each round has a time limit, and once the timer runs out, remaining players are forced all-in until opponents are knocked out. It was a difficult project with multiple pivots and major technical hurdles. We tested the designs directly with potential customers, specifically for clarity, usability, and whether the experience felt like something they'd want to play or that stood out from other poker products they'd tried. Two of the biggest changes we made came directly out of that testing: adjusting the pace of play, and redesigning how player seats looked and were placed at the table.

This project contributed to a USPTO design patent covering aspects of Electric Poker's interface.

Slot Portfolio Expansion

Sauce Boss BBQ

Responsible for

  • Designed the food-symbol collection and sell-out upgrade loop, mapping every bonus scenario, upgrade phase, and reset branch before implementation
  • Rebranded the reset tracker as “Bad Reviews,” adding thumbs-down stickers for 1-star hits so the three-review reset reads clearly at a glance
  • Created the “Sold Out” max-tier feedback pattern, with an arrow pointing to the higher-value symbol each collection upgrades into
  • Supported animation implementation by tuning sequence timing and transitions in close collaboration with art and engineering

Sauce Boss was built to drive our slot portfolio expansion — slots are the most popular driver of online casino play, and we needed to compete on variety and quality. It was our second in-house slot title, and it introduced a new collection mechanic: players collect and upgrade symbols to unlock bigger wins. The design challenge was fitting all of that collection state onto the screen in a way that fit the theme and actually made sense to the player.

Players collected a range of food symbols, and once a collection was fully “sold out,” the symbol upgraded to a higher-value food item. Star symbols ran as a separate system on the reels: three 1-star reviews triggered a negative reset of the collection, and 1–5 star ratings were mixed in alongside them. Early testing showed players couldn’t tell which stars were cosmetic and which were tracking against that reset, so they didn’t understand what they were actually collecting. We went through many iterations and, based on that testing, rebranded the tracker as “Bad Reviews,” with a thumbs-down sticker added each time a 1-star symbol landed, so the reset condition read clearly at a glance.

We also came up with the “Sold Out” idea for when a symbol collection hits its max tier: the UI shows “Sold Out” and draws an arrow pointing up to the higher-value food symbol it upgrades into — a piece of feedback that doubled as reinforcement of the food-truck theming.

Player Motivation

Dig!

Responsible for

  • Diagnosed low perceived reward in early levels from internal testing and restructured the opening sequence to surface the grand prize
  • Prototyped the new sequence as a Figma animatic to align stakeholders, then helped implement it in collaboration with art and engineering
  • Owned animation implementation and UI art layout implementation across the game
  • Tuned game timing and sequencing, from the opening reveal through the progression moments
  • Adjusted and implemented sound to support the pacing of each sequence
  • Set up the project in Unity, then exported and hard-coded the build for the Mezzanine engine

Dig! was our first in-house variety-type game: players dig down to uncover gems that equate to prizes, choosing to cash out along the way or risk it all for a shot at the final level and maximum prize. The main development hurdle was continuous choreography of character and environment sequencing as the player progressed.

We also had to revisit the opening sequence. Internal testing showed players felt like they weren't winning during the earlier levels and had no sense that the later levels held significantly more reward potential — the stakes at the final level were understated, so there was nothing pulling players to keep going. I personally led the effort to fix this: we storyboarded a new starting sequence that highlights the levels and clearly states the grand prize, and I animated it in Figma to show stakeholders, then helped implement it in collaboration with artists and engineers. It was a collaborative effort, and we set everything up in Unity, then exported and hard-coded the game for the Mezzanine engine, one of the last titles we shipped on it.

Shipped Dig! UI, including the revised opening sequence that surfaces the final-level grand prize.

3D / In Development

Chomped

Responsible for

  • Designed the latency-masking system: an unconditional shark dive, a server-driven resurface, and a graceful-close fallback for dropped connections
  • Led win/loss lane highlighting and chomp vs. escape animation choreography
  • Wrote the water shader and exposed its scripted parameters, tuning the look with the art lead
  • Built a mobile sub-menu system for secondary options and solved UIToolkit implementation for stylized concept text

Chomped is a new game similar to our most popular title, Rocket: players bet on up to 5 fish being chased by sharks, with the stake multiplying the longer they stay in. Wait too long to cash out and the fish gets Chomped — losing both winnings and stake. It's a translation of the Rocket mechanic into 3D, one of only a few fully 3D games in our portfolio, and another heavy cross-functional effort between art, design, and engineering to sequence the experience and find the pain points.

Mobile UI was one of the bigger challenges — there's a lot happening on screen at once, so we built a sub-menu system to move less-frequently-used options (like auto play settings) out of the primary view, then iterated on sizing for usability and tested it. We also hit implementation friction trying to replicate our artists' stylized concept text using UIToolkit, which needed some custom technical solutions to stay accurate to the original concepts.

As with Dig!, we adjusted animation sequencing and the “chomped” event choreography so outcomes read clearly as a win or a loss. I personally led that effort, adding lane highlighting for wins versus losses and directing distinct animations for a chomp versus an escape.

Because this is a live game, we also had to design around network latency. The shark always dives and disappears the instant a player hits cash out, regardless of what the server ends up returning — that part is unconditional, so there's no window where the animation has to "wait" on the network. It only resurfaces if the server's response is a loss, to play the chomp event; on a win, it simply never comes back. That decouples the dive from the round-trip entirely and masks latency inside an in-theme animation state rather than a loading spinner. If the connection drops outright rather than just lagging, the game falls back to our standard graceful-close procedure: the player sees a lost-connection message, and if the round was left in a broken state, they can still view the outcome afterward in their round history.

I also wrote the game's water shader myself, exposing it as a set of scripted parameters, and worked with our art lead to decide which variables to add, remove, or expose to fine-tune the look.

Design Systems & Leadership

Core Components

Responsible for

  • Built a reusable Figma component library and supported the Unity template project every new game starts from
  • Scripted a configurable chip selector in UIToolkit, in 2D and 3D, with parameters exposed for non-developers to tune
  • Defined the game recovery flow and screen-space guidelines for portrait and landscape
  • Mentored UX designers and aligned external development partners on UX patterns

Alongside shipping individual titles, I built out a library of reusable UI components in Figma — betting controls, screen-space guidelines for portrait and landscape, auto play controls, win celebration and messaging, game recovery flow, and options/toolbar patterns — which our art team then produced and engineering implemented as a template Unity project. Every new game on the team now starts from that template instead of from scratch.

The recovery flow specifically defines what a player sees if they return to a game after a connection drop or other failure. Depending on the game and where it left off, that can mean resuming an in-progress animation, returning to a static state, or, for live games where the moment has already passed, a message explaining the round's outcome or any events they missed.

For our chip-based games, I custom-scripted a highly configurable chip selector component, driven by UIToolkit, with both 2D and 3D versions. I set up the parameters and guides myself — chip counts, animation curves, fan-out spacing, selection scale — so non-developers (artists, producers, stakeholders) can tune the look and feel directly instead of requiring an engineering change for every visual tweak.

This system has had a tremendous impact on how quickly we can start a new game, and on getting stakeholders aligned on look and feel early in the process — it's also the same set of guidelines I use to mentor other UX designers and align external development partners on our UX patterns.
